# Labour Market Dualization, Permanent Insecurity and Fertility: The Case of Ultra-Low Fertility in South Korea

**Authors:**
Fleckenstein, T. and Lee, S.

**Year:**
2023

**Source:**
Population, Space and Place

**Dataset / Study Area:**
National statistics and survey data from South Korea, including fertility rates, labour market employment patterns, and attitudinal surveys (Statistics Korea; ISSP; Hankyoreh Institute; KIHASA).

---

## Research Objective

This study investigates how labour market dualization, the division between secure “insider” jobs and precarious “outsider” jobs, affects fertility in South Korea.

It focuses on understanding why South Korea exhibits ultra-low fertility (TFR 0.84 in 2020) despite extensive work-family policies and high female education, highlighting the roles of:

* permanent employment insecurity
* gendered labour market effects
* costs of childrearing linked to competitive education pressures

---

## Methodology

The study uses a macro-sociological approach combining:

* Labour market statistics (insider vs. outsider employment, wage gaps, tenure, training access)
* Fertility data (age-specific TFR, marriage rates, childlessness trends)
* Attitudinal surveys on perceived job insecurity, marriage intentions, and work-family expectations

Key analyses include:

* Mapping fertility decline in comparison with other OECD countries
* Examining gendered employment patterns and the distribution of non-standard work
* Linking perceptions of insecurity to fertility postponement, childlessness, and smaller family sizes

No explicit predictive modelling was used; the approach is descriptive and explanatory.

---

## Data

Key dataset features:

* **Fertility rates**: age-specific TFR from 1990s to 2020 (Statistics Korea)
* **Labour market**: proportion of non-standard and self-employed workers, wage gaps, training access
* **Surveys**: ISSP, Hankyoreh Institute, KIHASA (perceptions of job insecurity, marriage intentions, work-family attitudes)
* **Comparative context**: OECD data on employment, gender gaps, social protection

Highlights:

* 33% of employees in non-standard employment (21.2% temporary, 25.1% self-employed)
* Non-standard wages ~2/3 of standard wages; tenure 29 months vs. 89 months for insiders
* Only 40% of Korean workers reported job security (2005 survey)

---

## Key Findings

1. **Dualization depresses fertility** through multiple mechanisms:

    * Postponement of family formation for education and skill acquisition
    * Smaller family sizes and increased childlessness due to permanent insecurity
    * Fear of outsiderness affects insiders as well as outsiders

2. **Gendered impacts**:

    * Women disproportionately occupy outsider positions
    * Insider jobs are long hours, male-breadwinner oriented, incompatible with family responsibilities
    * High opportunity costs of childbearing reduce fertility

3. **Education and child costs**:

    * Intense competition for insider jobs creates “education fever”
    * Parents perceive high costs for raising children, reducing willingness to have multiple children

4. **Policy limitations**:

    * Work-family policies alone have not reversed fertility decline
    * Social protection gaps exacerbate perceived insecurity
    * Labour market reforms without addressing insecurity could worsen fertility

---

## Conclusion

Labour market dualization in South Korea is a critical driver of ultra-low fertility, interacting with gendered employment patterns and the high costs of childrearing.

* Fertility decline is not only due to delayed childbirth but also reduced quantum fertility and rising childlessness.
* Gender equity theories that assume women can combine work and family fail to account for dualized labour market effects.
* Effective policy must address permanent insecurity, not only reconcile work and family.

---

## Relevance to Our Project

* Supports using female labour market inactivity or outsider employment as a predictor for fertility.
* Demonstrates that fertility is influenced not only by economic resources but also by perceived employment insecurity and social norms.
* Highlights the importance of gendered labour market structures and long-term employment trajectories, relevant for modelling fertility in other low-fertility contexts.
