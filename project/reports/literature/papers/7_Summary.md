_Impacts of birthrate decline_

## Overview

* In 2023, the total fertility rate in England and Wales was 1.44 children per woman, the lowest on record. Because of declining fertility rates, and other factors such as people living longer, the UK has an ageing population.  
* Population changes can affect the number of people requiring services, and the number of people available to provide services, including in health and social care. They can also affect the number of younger and older people in the workforce.  
* Workforce changes may affect the economy, businesses and individual workers. Wider potential implications of birthrate decline may include effects on living standards, the provision of informal care, and the environment.  
* Some commentators suggest immigration can contribute to short-term increases in the total fertility rate. Some contend migration alone is not likely to play a significant role in alleviating pressures of an ageing population.  
* The global fertility rate has been declining almost continuously over the past 50 years. Some countries have implemented policies to improve family/work balance, which may support having and raising children. Evidence on the effectiveness of policies which seek to raise fertility is limited and contested.

## Objective

This paper provides contextual understanding of declining fertility trends and their broader economic and societal implications, rather than presenting a forecasting model.

It supports the motivation for forecasting birth rates, highlighting why accurate projections are important for planning labour markets, public services, and economic policy.

## Key Points

* The Total Fertility Rate (TFR) in England and Wales reached 1.44 in 2023, the lowest on record.
* Fertility decline contributes to population ageing, alongside increasing life expectancy.
* Declining birth rates can affect:
    * labour supply
    * economic growth
    * demand for healthcare and social care
    * dependency ratios
* Global fertility rates have been **declining for over 50 years**.
* Some countries have implemented **family support policies** (childcare support, parental leave) to address declining fertility.
* Evidence on whether such policies significantly **increase fertility rates remains mixed**.

## Models Used

This report does not implement forecasting models.
Instead, it combines evidence from demographic research, including:
* demographic projections
* population ageing models
* policy impact studies

## Relevance to Our Project

The paper highlights several structural consequences of declining fertility:

1. Ageing populations
2. Shrinking working-age population
3. Increased pressure on healthcare and pensions
4. Potential economic growth constraints

Although it does not provide modelling techniques, it strengthens the policy motivation for birth forecasting via:

* the importance of accurate demographic forecasting
* understanding the economic implications of fertility trends
* the need for evidence-based planning using population projections
