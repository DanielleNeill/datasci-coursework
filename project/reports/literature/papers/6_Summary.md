_Long-Term Fertility Trends by Birth Order in Britain: Comparison Between England & Wales and Scotland_

## Abstract

This study uses census-linked administrative data to investigate childbearing trends by birth order in Britain over three decades. This is the first study to investigate longer-term changes in fertility dynamics in Britain  by birth order and to compare parity-specific fertility by country. First-birth rates declined in the 1990s, slightly increased in the first decade of this century, and decreased thereafter, with changes in timing of parenthood responsible for these changes. Second- and third-birth rates declined in the 1990s but  remained relatively stable in the early twenty-first century. Birth intervals remained unchanged, meaning that changes in quantum are responsible for trends in higher-order birth rates. Time trends are similar in England & Wales and Scotland but with significantly lower second- and third-birth rates in Scotland.  Changes in population composition by education and ethnicity explain little of the aggregate fertility trends or between-country differences. Both countries have seen rapid declines in first-birth rates, especially among low-educated women.

## Objective

Provides a long-term decomposition of fertility trends, identifying structural changes driving declining births.

## Methods Used

"This study investigates childbearing trends in Britain—England & Wales and Scotland—by birth order over the last three decades. The novel contributions of the study are as follows. First, we determine whether changes in the TFR are attributable to changes in first, second, third, or higher-order births, or all of these. Second, we examine whether and how changes in fertility timing explain fertility trends by parity. Third, we compare fertility trends by parity in England & Wales vs Scotland. Finally, we conduct separate analyses by educational level and ethnic origin to understand whether and how changes in population composition explain fertility trends over time and differences across countries."

* Long-term demographic time-series analysis
* Birth-order decomposition
* Comparative analysis across Britain

## Best Performing Model

No predictive models used in this study - instead it used demographic decomposition methods.

Demographic decomposition methods are analytical techniques used to attribute changes in fertility rates to underlying demographic components such as birth order, age structure, and cohort behaviour, enabling researchers to identify the mechanisms driving observed fertility trends.

Even though our study will not use decomposition methods, they can help explain structural changes in birth data, such as:

* postponement of first births
* education-related fertility differences
* cohort behaviour

These structural shifts can affect time-series model performance, especially when trends change.

## Key Outcomes

* Fertility decline is strongly linked to declining first births.
* Fertility patterns differ between England & Wales and Scotland.
* Postponement of childbirth is a major contributor to declining fertility.

Helps interpret structural changes affecting birth time series, improving understanding of forecast dynamics.
